History

Chaturaji means "four kings" in Sanskrit, and it is one of the strangest branches on chess's family tree: a battle royale version of the game, four armies and four kings sharing a single board, with the outcome nudged along by a throw of the dice.

The First Written Trace

The earliest solid account of the game comes from the Persian scholar Al-Biruni, who described it around 1030 CE in his survey of Indian culture, science and customs, a work usually referred to in English as simply India. Al-Biruni describes four players, each commanding their own king and army, taking turns according to the roll of the dice rather than free choice.

There may be an even older echo of the game tucked into the Mahabharata, in a passage where a disguised prince boasts of his skill at dice, moving carved pawns of blue, yellow, red and white across a board. It's tantalizing, but not conclusive — scholars can't rule out that the verse is instead describing a race game like Pachisi rather than a chess relative.

Cousin, Not Ancestor

For a long stretch of the 1800s, the accepted story ran backwards from how historians see it today. British officer Hiram Cox, and later the scholar Duncan Forbes, argued that this four-player dice game was the primordial form of chess itself — that two-player Chaturanga, Persian Shatranj, and eventually modern chess all boiled down from Chaturaji once the dice were dropped and the four armies merged into two. It's a good story, and it held on for decades.

It didn't survive contact with H.J.R. Murray's monumental 1913 history of chess. Murray made the case that the relationship runs the other way: two-player Chaturanga came first, and this four-player, dice-driven form branched off from it later, likely by splitting one army into two pairs of opposing colors. Most chess historians today side with Murray. So despite the grand old theory, Chaturaji is chess's cousin, not its grandparent.

A Game That Kept Changing

The dice were oddly shaped — four-sided, oblong sticks rather than cubes, each face carrying a piece type instead of a number. Over time, in different regions and different centuries, that setup drifted: some players swapped in ordinary six-sided dice, and by the close of the 19th century a diceless, purely skill-based version was reportedly still being played in parts of India, the randomness dropped entirely in favor of free choice of piece.

The game nearly vanished from living play in the 20th century, kept alive mostly in the footnotes of chess history books. It's had a small digital revival recently — a version was added to Chess.com's variants library in 2022–2023, using a points-based ending rather than the older sudden-death, last-king-standing finish.

This Build

This game leans back toward the older, dice-driven spirit of Al-Biruni's account rather than the modern points-based version: four armies, oblong dice reduced to two simple four-siders, and a game that ends the moment only one king is still breathing. One liberty has been taken with the classical setup on purpose — more on that in Piece Meanings.

How to Play

Objective

Four armies, one board. Keep your king alive while the other three kingdoms fall, and you win. There's no check and no checkmate here — a king can be captured outright, just like any other piece, so its safety is entirely on you.

Setup & Turn Order

Each of the four corners holds one army of eight: a King, an Elephant, a Horse, a Boat, and four Pawns. Turn order runs counter-clockwise around the board — Red, Green, Black, Yellow — and Red always opens the game.

Rolling for Your Move

On your turn you roll two four-sided dice. Each die lands on one of four faces — King, Elephant, Horse, or Boat — and each face is a token letting you move one piece of that matching type.

  • Got two different faces? Move one piece for each.
  • Rolled doubles? You may move that same piece twice in a row, once per token, if it still has a legal second move from its new square.
  • Is the piece that face calls for already captured? Move a pawn instead.
  • Rolled a King? You may move the King one step — or move a pawn instead, your choice.
  • No legal move available for a token at all? It's simply wasted; play continues.
  • You may also skip a token voluntarily if every legal option looks like a bad idea.

How Each Piece Moves

  • King — one square in any direction, just like a chess king.
  • Elephant — slides any distance along a rank or file, exactly like a chess rook. It stops dead at the first piece in its path, friend or foe.
  • Horse — the familiar L-shaped knight jump, hopping clean over anything in between.
  • Boat — leaps exactly two squares along a diagonal, hopping over whatever sits on the square between (it doesn't matter who's there). It can land on an empty square or capture an enemy piece — except an enemy king. The Boat is the one piece on the board that can never deliver the final blow to a king.
  • Pawn — one square straight ahead in its own forward direction, but only into empty ground; it never captures by moving straight. Diagonally forward, it's the reverse: it can only move there to capture an enemy piece. No opening two-square dash, no capturing en passant.

Every army's pawns face a different direction — north, south, east or west — angled to march toward the far side of the board rather than straight at any one neighbor.

Promotion

When one of your pawns reaches the far edge of the board, it doesn't become a queen — there isn't one here. Instead, it calls a fallen comrade back into the fight: choose one previously-captured Elephant, Horse, or Boat of your own color, and your pawn is instantly replaced by that piece, right there on the promotion square. If none of your major pieces have fallen yet, the pawn simply has nowhere left to go and sits at the edge.

Scoring

Every capture pays out points based on what you took: King 5, Elephant 4, Horse 3, Boat 2, Pawn 1. Capturing a king pays out more than just its own 5, though — the instant a king falls, every piece still standing in that kingdom is swept from the board as well, and its point value is added straight onto your total as a surplus. Wipe out a kingdom that still has an Elephant and two Pawns guarding it, for example, and you collect 5 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 11 points in one stroke. Scores are tracked for bragging rights and shown for all four players throughout the game — but survival still decides the actual winner, not the scoreboard.

Winning

The moment only one king remains standing anywhere on the board, that player's kingdom is the last one standing, and the game is over. As a backstop against an overly cautious standoff, if 80 moves pass in a row with no capture from anyone, the game is called a stalemate.

Piece Meanings

Every piece in Chaturaji traces back to a division of an ancient Indian army. The Sanskrit term chaturanga, "four limbs," names the four traditional wings of a royal military: chariots, cavalry, elephants and infantry — and those four wings are exactly where chess's own pieces, on both the two-player and four-player boards, ultimately come from.

A Deliberate House Rule

One thing in this build breaks from the historical record on purpose. In Al-Biruni's original layout, and in most reconstructions since, the Boat sits on the true corner square, with the King pushed inward next to the pawns — the piece with the least to fear guarding the safest square, and the piece with the most to lose standing further out. Here, that's flipped: the King now occupies the actual corner, with Elephant, Horse and Boat screening it in front, one behind the other. It's a small change, but it rewards patient defense rather than punishing the one piece you can least afford to lose.

Egyptian Gods leans on the same logic occultists have used for over a century in Enochian Chess, a Golden Dawn system that maps Egyptian deities onto chess pieces via the letters of the Tetragrammaton. Osiris anchors the King's corner wearing the Atef crown; Horus, falcon-headed, takes the Horse's leaping move; Thoth, ibis-headed god of writing and reckoning, takes the Boat's diagonal hop; Nephthys, classically paired with the rook-like piece in the Enochian correspondence table, takes the sliding Elephant; and Isis, crowned with the solar disk between horns, marches as the Pawn — fittingly, since she's the one piece capable of calling a fallen god back into the fight.

Hebrew Letters renders each piece as a single letter rather than a silhouette: Shin (fire, one of the three "mother letters") for the King, Heh for the Elephant, Yod for the Horse, Vav for the Boat, and Aleph — the first letter, breath, beginnings — for the Pawn.

Strategy & Variants

Tips for the Table

  • Guard the approach, not just the King. With the King tucked in the corner, the real fight is over the Elephant, Horse and Boat standing in front of it — lose all three and the King is suddenly playing alone.
  • The Boat is a harasser, not a finisher. It can hop past blockers and pick off loose pawns or stranded pieces at range, but remember it can never take a king itself — line up a different piece for the kill.
  • Watch who's winning, not just your own board. In a four-way free-for-all, the player currently ahead is everyone else's problem. Piling on together against the strongest kingdom, even with no formal alliance, is often the correct move.
  • Race pawns toward promotion once a major piece falls. A resurrected Elephant or Horse can completely swing a game that looked lost.
  • Don't hoard doubles. Moving one piece twice feels efficient, but it can leave the rest of your army exactly where it started while opponents develop.

Advanced Tips (From the Table)

A few more that only really show up after a lot of sessions:

  • Open with the Boat, finish with the Horse. The Boat's diagonal hop is built for prying open an outer wall of pawns and screening pieces since it ignores whatever sits between it and its landing square. Once that outer wall has a gap in it, the Horse's L-shaped jump is what actually threads through to a target sitting deeper in the formation.
  • Don't spend pawns carelessly. Unlike ordinary chess, pawns here are never the "expendable" piece. By the endgame, once most major pieces on the board have already fallen, a pawn's ability to march to the edge and resurrect a dead Elephant, Horse, or Boat can matter more than almost anything else in play. Trade them like you'd trade a real piece, not a spare.
  • The Elephant is one of the most efficient king-killers. Because it slides the full length of an open rank or file, rolling doubles on your Elephant hands you two full sweeps of the board in a single turn — often enough on its own to run down an exposed king.
  • Watch your open lines late-game. As pieces clear off the board, any enemy Elephant sitting anywhere on your king's rank or file suddenly has a clean shot straight at it. Once material starts thinning out, keep at least one piece parked somewhere on your king's own rank and file to break that line before it becomes an ambush.

CPU Difficulty, Explained

  • Easy — plays reasonably but loosely, leans toward captures when it sees one, and won't always spot danger coming.
  • Medium — consistently takes the best capture available and checks whether its own piece is walking into a trade first.
  • Hard — looks a full move ahead for every option, weighing captures, counter-captures, and threats to its own king before choosing.
  • Grand Master — everything Hard does, plus it favors flexible, mobile piece placement, actively guards its own king's exposure rather than just reacting to it, and presses harder on kingdoms that are already down to a few pieces to finish them off for the elimination bonus.

Historical Variants

The dice-driven, every-king-for-himself version built here is only one branch of how Chaturaji has been played over the centuries. A few others worth knowing:

  • Partnership play. Some historical rule sets seat the four players as two fixed teams, diagonal partner playing diagonal partner, cooperating to protect both allied kings and jointly ruling an "empire" if they eliminate both opponents.
  • The four-boat square. One classical rule holds that if a single player somehow maneuvers all four of their own boats (or, in some readings, a boat surrounded by three others) into the four corners of one empty 2×2 block, that formation instantly captures every other boat on the board. It's a wonderfully strange rule, and it's deliberately left out here in favor of the simpler dice game.
  • Scoring as the win condition. This build tracks points (see How to Play) but still crowns whoever survives. Several historical and modern rule sets instead end the game as soon as three kings have fallen and crown whoever has the highest score at that point, win or lose on the board.

Collectibles

Every completed game casts a geomantic figure from the final standings: rank the four scores highest to lowest, read each one as Odd or Even, and that four-line sequence points to one of the sixteen classical figures — the same system diviners have used for centuries, just fed by dice and kingdoms instead of sand or a pen. Win, lose, or stalemate, a figure is always cast and added below.

Mission Statement & Goals

The purpose of digitizing Chaturaji is to bridge centuries of strategy by placing the ancient world directly at the fingertips of the modern player. By translating this classic four-player ancestor of chess into an intuitive, accessible digital experience, we make medieval gaming culture vibrant and engaging for today's audience.

The Three Goals

  • Heritage & Accessibility — Strip away the barrier of obscure rules and specialized equipment, making an ancient Indian gaming tradition instantly playable for casual players and strategy enthusiasts alike.
  • Modernized Mechanics — Enhance the traditional dice-rolling and four-army dynamics with dynamic visuals, real-time board feedback, and balanced matchmaking without losing the game's historical core.
  • Social Play — Elevate the four-player, dynamic nature of Chaturaji into a vibrant digital battlefield where friends and global opponents can test their foresight, alliances, and adaptability.
